# SCRIPT — EN-L01

**Título de trabajo:** AI Just Automated the $150K Job Nobody Saw Coming
**Canal:** Market Autopsy (EN)
**Pilar:** Pilar 2 — "AI Disruption Watch"
**Duración objetivo:** 13–15 min
**Tono:** Documental, voz en off calmada y autoritaria, basado en datos, sin hype

**Nota de investigación:** este guion usa hechos públicos y verificables (estudio LawGeex 2018, reporte Goldman Sachs 2023, financiamiento de Harvey AI) como columna vertebral de la historia. Antes de grabar, verifica cada cifra citada abajo contra la fuente original más reciente disponible — los números de adopción de IA en el sector legal cambian rápido y algunos aquí son de 2023-2024.

---

## 0:00 — HOOK

> In 2018, a machine read fifty legal contracts in twenty-six seconds.
> Twenty lawyers took ninety-two minutes to do the same job.
> The machine caught more mistakes.
>
> That test happened seven years ago. Today, that machine has a job.
> And the $200,000-a-year career it was hired to replace? It's disappearing —
> not in some future you're supposed to worry about. Right now.

*(Visual: split screen — stopwatch counting 26 seconds vs. 92 minutes; stack of contracts)*

## 0:15 — PROMESA

> By the end of this video, you'll know exactly which job AI already automated,
> why nobody saw it coming — including the people whose job it was — and what
> it tells us about the next ten professions on the list.

## 0:30 — CONTEXTO

> The job is: first-year corporate lawyer. Specifically, the part of the job
> that pays the bills for every big law firm on the planet — contract review
> and document discovery.
>
> If you've never worked in law, here's what you need to know: becoming a
> lawyer at a top firm is one of the most competitive, most expensive paths
> in the professional world. Four years of college, three years of law
> school, six figures of debt — all to compete for a starting salary that,
> at the top firms, now sits above $200,000 a year.
>
> And for the first two to three years, a huge chunk of that job isn't
> arguing cases or advising clients. It's reading. Reading contracts,
> reading depositions, reading thousands of pages of documents, looking
> for the one clause that changes everything.

*(Visual: montage — law school graduation, office towers, stacks of paper, tired associate at 2am)*

## 1:00 — PROBLEMA

> Here's the part that made this job perfect for a machine to take: it was
> never really about judgment. It was about pattern-matching, at massive
> scale, without getting tired.
>
> A junior associate at a top firm can bill $400 to $900 an hour of a
> client's money — reading documents a computer can read for free. That
> gap didn't exist because the work required a human. It existed because,
> until recently, nothing else could do it.
>
> That changed faster than almost anyone in the industry expected.

## 2:00 — DESARROLLO

> In 2018, legal tech company LawGeex ran a study that the legal industry
> is still talking about. They took twenty experienced lawyers — some with
> over twenty years of experience — and had them review five standard
> non-disclosure agreements, hunting for legal risks.
>
> Then they had an AI system do the same task.
>
> The AI's average accuracy: 94%.
> The lawyers' average accuracy: 85%.
>
> The AI finished in 26 seconds.
> The lawyers took an average of 92 minutes.
>
> That's not "AI is almost as good as a junior lawyer." That's AI being
> *more accurate* and *over 200 times faster* — on the exact task that
> generates a huge share of billable hours for every corporate law firm.

*(Open loop: "So if the tech existed in 2018, why did it take until now to actually change the industry? That's where it gets interesting.")*

> The honest answer is: law is slow to change on purpose. Client
> confidentiality, malpractice liability, and a culture built on
> precedent meant firms spent years testing, not deploying.
>
> But two things broke that hesitation.
>
> First: large language models. The 2018 tool was narrow — trained to
> spot specific clauses in specific document types. The new generation,
> built on the same technology behind ChatGPT, can read almost any
> document, in almost any format, and explain its reasoning in plain
> English. That's the difference between a calculator and an intern.
>
> Second: the money noticed. In 2022, a legal AI startup called Harvey —
> yes, that's really the name — raised funding backed by OpenAI's own
> startup fund. By 2024, Harvey was valued at over $1.5 billion, with
> customers including some of the largest law firms in the world:
> Allen & Overy, PwC, over a hundred firms in total using it to review
> contracts, research case law, and draft first versions of documents
> that used to take junior associates all night.
>
> When money moves that fast into one narrow use case, that's the
> market telling you something got automated for real.

*(Visual: funding headline graphics, law firm logos, before/after workflow diagram)*

## TURNING POINT — ~7:30

> Here's the part that should make every white-collar professional pay
> attention, not just lawyers.
>
> In 2023, Goldman Sachs published a report estimating that generative
> AI could eventually automate tasks equivalent to 300 million full-time
> jobs globally — and legal work ranked among the professions with the
> *highest* exposure, alongside other document-heavy, judgment-light
> knowledge work.
>
> Not because lawyers are replaceable. Because a specific, high-paying
> *layer* of the job — the part that trained AI on decades of contracts
> and case law could learn to imitate — was more exposed than anyone
> in the profession wanted to admit.
>
> And here's the twist almost nobody talks about: the lawyers didn't
> lose their jobs overnight. The *job itself* changed shape. Law firms
> aren't hiring fewer junior associates because they suddenly need less
> legal work done — they're hiring fewer because one associate with AI
> tools can now do the document review work that used to take a team of
> five.
>
> That's not a story about unemployment. That's a story about leverage —
> and leverage always finds the same pattern: it makes a few people at
> the top far more productive, and it makes the entry-level version of
> that job disappear.

*(Visual: org chart shrinking at the bottom, staying the same at the top)*

## DESARROLLO (continuación) — ~9:30

> This is the pattern worth remembering, because it's not unique to law.
>
> Radiologists faced the same headline a few years earlier — "AI reads
> X-rays better than doctors" — and the job didn't vanish either. It
> changed. AI now does the first pass on routine scans in many hospitals;
> radiologists spend more time on complex cases and less on routine
> screening.
>
> Financial analysts are watching the same shift happen to equity
> research summaries. Customer support saw it happen to tier-1 tickets
> years ago.
>
> The jobs most exposed share three traits: high volume of documents or
> data, well-defined patterns, and a paper trail of past examples large
> enough to train a model on. If your job has all three, you're not
> looking at "someday." You're looking at "already happening,
> somewhere, to someone with your exact title."

## CONCLUSIÓN — ~12:00

> So what actually happened to the $150,000-a-year job? It didn't
> disappear in a headline. It got quietly absorbed into a tool that
> costs a law firm a subscription fee instead of a salary — and the
> handful of junior lawyers still getting hired are the ones who know
> how to direct that tool, check its work, and handle the judgment
> calls it still can't make.
>
> That's the real shape of this disruption, industry after industry:
> not "the job is gone," but "the job now requires one-tenth the
> headcount, and the other nine-tenths need a different skill than the
> one they trained for."
